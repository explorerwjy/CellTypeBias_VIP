**Reviewer \#1**: In this paper the authors take a multivariate approach
to addressing the intersection of gene mutation to neuropsychiatric
disease. They begin by taking human data on ASD from the Spark
consortium and Scz data from the Schema consortium. Cross analyzing this
data with analysis of human cell type gene expression data, they
conclude a swath of cell types including eccentric MSNs, Lamp5-Lhx6
populations and chandelier neurons, CGE neurons, Upper and deep layer IT
neurons and finally Midbrain inhibitory cells are strongly affected by
the intersection of the genes implicated jointly from the Spark and
Schema data sets. In addition they identify several super clusters
including excitatory amygdala, hippocampus and both IT and ET
populations and say these findings are consistent to with such brain
disorders. In effect, they have implicated a large portion of the cell
types in both pallial and subpallial structures as being candidate for
being impacted in patients suffering from either ASD and SCZ. Going on
from there they then focus down on MGE and CGE-derived cells with a
particular focus on Lamp5-Lhx6/Chandelier, which comprise a tiny
subpopulation of the broader interneuron subtypes. From this more
general vantage, they switch their focus to the 22q11.2 deletion
disorder and use the Df(16)A+/- mouse model to study this in mice. Using
a largely motor based metric, they conclude that VIP interneurons are
disproportionately affected in their function, leading them to generally
conclude this implicates VIP interneurons as the underlying cause of
certain forms of ASD/SCZ.

Taken together this work provides a large survey of genes, cell types
and psychiatric conditions but feel more like a review article than a
focused piece attempting to link gene defects to an underlying cause.
While the work seems to be carefully assembled, the broad overview does
little to uncover a distinct subtype being convincingly implicated in
the disease rather than simply providing hints as to a large number of
cell types that may be implicated. As such, while aspects of this work
are of interest, they collectively short fall far of providing a
convincing narrative to implicate any cell type, the VIP one
investigated in their Df(16)A+/- mouse model in particular, as being
centrally involved in ASD and SCZ disorders. Given the wide range of
neuropsychiatric disorders they are grouping, this isn\'t terribly
surprising and does not argue strongly at all that this work is suitable
for publication in Neuron.

**Reply Plan:** We sincerely thank the Reviewer for their thoughtful
comments and for the opportunity to clarify and sharpen the narrative
and goals of our study. Rare, highly penetrant variants are known to be
strong drivers of cognitive phenotypes across neuropsychiatric
disorders. The recent emergence of comprehensive human single-cell
atlases from the Allen Institute provides an unprecedented opportunity
to investigate the neuronal consequences of such rare mutations in a
systematic and unbiased manner. While prior studies have largely focused
on common-variant GWAS signals, the cell-type specificity of rare,
high-impact mutations has remained comparatively unexplored.

To address this gap, we developed a computational framework to
systematically identify human neuron types selectively impacted by rare
mutations. This analysis reveals that, although multiple neuron types
are affected, caudal ganglionic eminence (CGE)--derived GABAergic
interneurons emerge as a consistent and specific nexus of *cognitive
phenotypes* across ASD, schizophrenia, developmental delay,
verbal--numerical reasoning phenotypes in the UK Biobank, and 22q11.2
deletion syndrome. This convergence distinguishes CGE-derived
interneurons from both MGE-derived interneurons and excitatory
projection neurons, and identifies a cross-disorder cellular axis of
cognitive vulnerability that, to our knowledge, has not been previously
recognized.

We agree with the Reviewer that the original presentation placed
disproportionate emphasis on atlas-level mapping. In the revised
manuscript, we will substantially reorganize the Introduction and
Results to streamline the narrative and pivot earlier toward the central
finding that CGE-derived interneurons represent a shared locus of
vulnerability for cognitive dysfunction across disorders.

Within this CGE framework, our *in vivo* work focuses on CGE/VIP
interneuron populations because they provide a mechanistically
interpretable and experimentally accessible instantiation of the broader
CGE lineage signal. Although numerically smaller, these interneurons
play a disproportionate role in regulating cortical gain, contextual
modulation, and information routing. Notably, the phenotypes we
investigate *in vivo* are specific to VIP interneuron intrinsic
excitability and their encoding of spatial and reward-related
information, indicating a disruption in cognitive representation rather
than movement. In the revised manuscript, we will clarify this point and
reposition the mouse data to emphasize its role in linking rare mutation
burden to circuit-level dysfunction.

Taken together, the revised manuscript will present a focused,
hypothesis-driven investigation rather than a broad survey. By
integrating large-scale human genetics with single-cell transcriptomics
and targeted circuit-level physiology, our study identifies a convergent
cellular mechanism through which rare, high-impact mutations impair
cognition across neuropsychiatric conditions. We believe this refined
narrative more clearly conveys the mechanistic insight and conceptual
advance of the work and aligns well with the scope and standards of
*Neuron*.

whether there is a biased enrichment of genes identified as rare, highly

3\) It is clear from this paper that the activity of VIP interneurons is

**Reply Plan**: We agree with the Reviewer that the causal link between
altered VIP interneuron activity and disinhibition of pyramidal neurons
should be carefully framed. In the current manuscript, we do not claim
direct experimental proof of PV/SOM disinhibition; rather, we interpret
our findings within a well-established circuit framework in which VIP
interneurons preferentially inhibit SOM and PV interneurons. In the
revision, we will:

-   Explicitly acknowledge this limitation in the Results and
    Discussion, and clarify that our data demonstrate VIP interneuron
    dysfunction and impaired spatial coding, which are consistent with,
    but do not directly prove, a disinhibitory mechanism.

-   Cite prior work (including our previous *Nature Neuroscience* study
    and related analyses of PV/SOM dynamics) as converging evidence
    supporting this circuit interpretation.

While we agree that optogenetic rescue experiments activating VIP
interneurons would, in principle, provide a powerful test of causality,
such experiments fall beyond the scope of the present study and will be
clearly identified as an important direction for future work.
Importantly, the interpretability of such rescue experiments in the

optogenetic activation of VIP interneurons may not fully restore normal
circuit function when dozens of dosage-sensitive genes are
simultaneously affected. Moreover, PV and SOM interneurons are
themselves perturbed by the same deletion, further complicating causal
attribution and necessitating extensive additional control experiments.
Addressing these interacting circuit-level effects would require a
substantial experimental expansion beyond the focus of the current
manuscript, and we will explicitly discuss these limitations in the
revised version.

**Reply Plan**: We thank the Reviewer for this question, and we agree

currently difficult, due to two fundamental issues:

-   Marker ambiguity (mRNA versus protein): While immunohistochemical
    markers such as CCK and CR provide discrete subtype labels in mice,

evolutionarily conserved "VIP+ CGE" supercluster as the most robust and
biologically meaningful unit for cross-species genetic integration.

We thank the Reviewer for the careful, balanced, and insightful
evaluation of our manuscript. We appreciate the recognition of the
ambition and technical rigor of the work, as well as the thoughtful
guidance on how to more clearly articulate the scope and interpretation
of our findings. We fully agree that manuscripts bridging large-scale
computational inference with targeted circuit-level interrogation must
be especially careful in framing their conclusions. In the revision, we
will expand the discussion to explore non-VIP effects in circuit,
including the results of our previous work examining other interneuron
subtypes. Below, we address each of the Reviewer's points in turn.

Major points:

1\. Validation of the computational pipeline

The permutation framework needs stronger justification. Matching only on
gene set size is insufficient; at minimum, random gene sets should be
matched on gene length, conservation, and expression level. The authors
should more comprehensively validate whether their \"mutation bias\"
statistic is robust to such gene-level confounds.

**Reply Plan**: We thank the Reviewer for this insightful comment. We
agree that it is important to carefully justify the permutation
framework and to examine potential gene-level confounders. We note that
the two confounders mentioned, gene length and expression level, are
already accounted for in our mutation-bias calculation. Specifically, we
subtract the expected mutation counts conditional on gene length; we
will clarify this point in the revision. Expression level is controlled
at two stages: (i) TPM-based normalization across cell types (Equation
1), which prevents cell types with higher total UMI counts from ranking
higher, and (ii) gene-level specificity scores that normalize TPM values
across all cell types (Equation 2), which prevents highly expressed
genes from disproportionately dominating the bias. In the revision, we
will further investigate the effects of gene-level confounders,
including gene length and evolutionary conservation.

2\. Gene set size and stability of estimates

In Figure S4, mutation bias correlations remain stable even when the SCZ
gene set is expanded to 200 genes. This is surprising: including genes
without true association should add noise. Why is this not the case? The
statistical explanation for this stability needs to be clarified. More
generally, the manuscript should explicitly address how the number of
included genes affects the stability of their estimates. Why should the
number of genes be selected based on number of ASD rare-variant
implicated genes?

**Reply Plan**: We thank the Reviewer for this comment. We agree with
the Reviewer that the stability of the mutation bias upon expansion of
the SCZ gene set requires further investigation. Our preliminary
analyses indicate that the previously reported stability observed after
adding 200 genes reflects overall mutation-bias patterns, rather than
cell-type--specific effects (such as those involving CGE interneurons).
Indeed, it would have been surprising if cell-type specificity were
preserved after the inclusion of genes without true disease
associations. We will investigate this issue further and clarify these
points explicitly in the revised manuscript.

3\. Impact of genetic architecture

The authors should explain how differences in genetic architecture (rare
de novo-driven in ASD vs. modest case-control burden in SCZ) impact the
statistical properties of the mutation bias metric. What is the minimum
signal needed for robust inference? Is there a minimum sample size of
the rare-variant genetic investigation at which the mutational bias is
stable? Consider including a set of negative control traits, such as
blood traits or other rare-variant investigations into non-brain related
traits.

**Reply Plan:** To investigate this interesting question raised by the
Reviewer, we will perform downsampling of the available mutation data.
We will also apply our pipeline to non--brain-related control traits
from the UK Biobank (e.g., HDL cholesterol, Alanine aminotransferase,
inflammatory bowel disease, Red blood cell distribution width),
demonstrating the absence of neuronal enrichment and thereby
establishing specificity for psychiatric and cognitive phenotypes.

4\. Treatment of cognitive phenotypes (VNR)

The inclusion of verbal-numerical reasoning (VNR) is a good idea.
However, splitting genes by positive vs. negative effect sizes is
misleading. Due to negative selection, large-effect positive alleles on
cognition are extremely rare. Thus, VNR(+) is likely a \"null\" set
without signal. The authors do not split ASD or SCZ by direction, so why
should VNR be split?

**Reply Plan**: We agree with the Reviewer that the VNR(+) gene set
serves effectively as an internal negative control, and we will revise
the text accordingly. We will also evaluate additional null gene sets
for VNR(-), demonstrating that our results are robust across different
choices of null gene sets. Notably, our preliminary analysis of
schizophrenia (SCZ) genes with protective effects (odds ratio \< 1)
indicates that these genes are depleted in CGE interneuron specificity.
We will include this result in the revised manuscript.

5\. Interpretation of differential bias tests

The authors highlight CGE interneurons as differentially biased across
disorders, but many comparisons rely on small numbers of significantly
associated clusters. For example, in ASD without ID, only 7 cell types
pass significance, none of which are CGE. How do the authors justify
making differential claims when one side of the comparison lacks clear
signal?

**Reply Plan**: We apologize for any potential confusion. The absence of
a significant CGE bias in ASD without intellectual disability (ID)
indicates that CGE interneurons are not preferentially impacted in this
condition. This result provides a biologically meaningful near-zero
baseline signal and contrasts, in a statistically significant manner,
with ASD with ID and other cognitively impaired phenotypes. Other
neuronal classes do not exhibit this pattern, and CGE interneurons
emerge as the most discriminative neuronal population when comparing
mutation biases associated with cognitive impairment versus those
without cognitive impairment across disorders. We will clarify this
point further in the revised manuscript.
